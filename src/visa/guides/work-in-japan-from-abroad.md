<h2 id="tourist">Coming to Japan as a tourist generally does not allow you to work</h2>

People who come to Japan under the Temporary Visitor status, such as for tourism, generally cannot engage in paid employment. You may be able to attend job interviews during a short-term stay, but that is different from actually working. Also, changing from Temporary Visitor to a status of residence that permits employment is not approved unless there are unavoidable special circumstances.

Even if you are from a country or region whose citizens do not need a visa to visit Japan, the visa exemption covers only short-term stays. To work in Japan long-term, you need a status of residence that permits work.

If you want to work in Japan, you generally need to find a job and complete the procedures while you are still outside Japan.

<h2 id="process">The process for working in Japan from abroad</h2>

1. **Find a job in Japan**<br>Some companies hold interviews online.
2. **Apply for a Certificate of Eligibility in Japan**<br>A Certificate of Eligibility (COE) confirms in advance that your planned activities in Japan meet part of the conditions for entering Japan. When the applicant lives abroad, an employee of the hiring company or another authorized representative usually applies to the Immigration Services Agency in Japan.
3. **Receive the Certificate of Eligibility**<br>It can also be issued by email. If it is issued by email, the company can forward it to you, so you do not need to wait for it to be mailed abroad. A COE is generally valid for three months from the date of issue. Apply for your visa so that you can enter Japan within that period.
4. **Apply for a visa, as a rule at the Japanese embassy or consulate with jurisdiction over your place of residence**<br>In some countries, you must apply through a designated visa application center.
5. **Come to Japan, receive Landing Permission at immigration, then start work**<br>You can only work within the scope of the status of residence you are granted.

Receiving a COE does not guarantee that a visa will be issued or that you will be allowed to enter Japan. The process is also different for some statuses and programs, such as the Working Holiday program.

### The difference between a "visa" and a "status of residence"

Outside Japan, people often use the word "visa" for the right to live and work in Japan as well. In Japan, the two are separate.

- **Visa**: Issued by a Japanese embassy or consulate, a visa is one of the requirements for entering Japan for people who need one. Having a visa does not guarantee entry
- **Status of residence**: The legal status that allows you to stay and carry out activities in Japan. It is usually granted upon receiving Landing Permission, and in some cases it can be changed after entry

<h2 id="status">Which status of residence might fit you?</h2>

The main statuses and programs used to come and work in Japan are below. See also [Japan Visa Types](/visa/guides/japan-visa-types/).

- **Engineer/Specialist in Humanities/International Services**: Professional work that requires specialized knowledge or skills, such as interpreting and translation, overseas sales, marketing and engineering. As a rule, there are requirements such as relevant educational qualifications or work experience
- **Specified Skilled Worker (i)**: Work in fields with labor shortages, such as nursing care, food service and manufacturing. As a rule, you must pass a skills test for the field and a Japanese-language test. The tests and the required level of Japanese differ by field. People who have successfully completed Technical Intern Training (ii) may be exempt from the tests if they meet the conditions. In some countries and fields, you can take the tests outside Japan
- **Working Holiday**: A program that lets young people from countries and regions with an agreement with Japan enjoy a holiday and work to help cover their living costs. There are conditions such as nationality and age

From April 1, 2027, a new program called Employment for Skill Development (ESD) will start, replacing the Technical Intern Training Program.

<h2 id="search">How can you find a job?</h2>

You can search for jobs at Japanese companies yourself. Use the career pages of Japanese companies that actively hire foreign nationals, and job websites that accept applications from abroad. However, depending on your status and country, some procedures, such as going through a sending organization, may be required.

Japanese language skills expand the range of jobs you can apply for. We recommend taking the Japanese-Language Proficiency Test (JLPT) or the Japan Foundation Test for Basic Japanese (JFT-Basic), which is also used for Specified Skilled Worker (i) applications, to demonstrate your Japanese level.

<h2 id="agents">Be careful with agents who offer jobs in Japan</h2>

Outside Japan, there are sending organizations and brokers that offer jobs in Japan. Some of them ask for very high fees. In a survey of technical intern trainees published by the Immigration Services Agency in 2022, about 55% of respondents had borrowed money before coming to Japan, and the average debt among those who reported the amount borrowed was about ¥550,000.

Be careful with agents who:

- ask for a deposit as a condition for working in Japan, or make you agree to a penalty if you quit the job
- promise that you will "definitely get a visa"
- try to keep your passport without a valid reason, or do not return it
- do not explain the breakdown of fees in writing, or do not give you a contract or receipts
- do not tell you who the employer in Japan is or what the job is
- ask you to provide false information about your education or work experience
- tell you that you can work if you come to Japan on a tourist visa

If you use an agent, check that it has the necessary licenses or approvals. For Technical Intern Training, Specified Skilled Worker and similar programs, some countries require you to use a sending organization under a government-to-government agreement or to complete other procedures.

Under the Employment for Skill Development (ESD) program starting in April 2027, the total fees a worker in the supervised type of the program pays during the sending process will be capped at two months of the monthly remuneration stated in their ESD plan. For example, if the monthly remuneration is ¥200,000, the cap is ¥400,000. Fees paid to educational institutions and intermediaries that effectively work as one with the sending organization also count toward this cap. Any amount above the cap cannot be charged to the worker. This is not a cap on all costs of coming to Japan.
