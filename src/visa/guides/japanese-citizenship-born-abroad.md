## The nationality rule many parents overlook

Under U.S. law, a child born in the United States is generally a U.S. citizen at birth. On June 30, 2026, the U.S. Supreme Court held that a child born in the United States is generally a U.S. citizen at birth, even if the parents are in the country unlawfully or only temporarily.

Japanese nationality follows a different principle: it passes through a parent, not the place of birth. If the father or mother is a Japanese national when the child is born, the child is, in principle, also a Japanese national. However, the outcome can differ depending on factors such as the legal parent-child relationship at the time of birth. A child who is also Japanese is born holding two nationalities.

This may seem straightforward, but there is an important deadline. When a Japanese child is born abroad and acquires another nationality at birth, the parents must file a **nationality reservation (国籍留保)** together with the birth report **within 3 months of the date of birth**. It can be filed at a Japanese embassy or consulate, or at a municipal office in Japan.

If the reservation is not filed in time, Japanese law treats the child as having lost Japanese nationality retroactive to birth. The deadline is extended only in limited cases — for example, when a natural disaster or another cause beyond the parents' control made filing impossible. In that case, the period becomes 14 days from the time filing becomes possible.

## Why families may miss the deadline

Three months can pass quickly after a birth. New parents are dealing with recovery, medical appointments and caring for a newborn, and a consulate filing called "nationality reservation" is easy to overlook.

Some Japanese parents also assume that Japanese nationality continues automatically for any child born to a Japanese parent. For a child born abroad who acquires another nationality at birth, it does not.

## If your child is already an adult, this may still matter

Some people born in the U.S. to a Japanese parent first look into their Japanese nationality in their 20s or 30s — for example, when they consider moving to Japan, apply for a Japanese passport, or check their parent's family register (戸籍).

The next steps depend on the situation:

- **The reservation was not filed within 3 months.** A person who lost Japanese nationality this way can reacquire it by notification to the Minister of Justice, but only while under 18 and living in Japan. The notification is filed in person at a Legal Affairs Bureau by the person (or, if under 15, by a parent or other legal representative). If the person is 18 or older, or does not live in Japan, this notification is not possible, and other options such as naturalization (帰化) need to be considered. Some naturalization requirements are relaxed for children of Japanese nationals who live in Japan.
- **Nationality was properly reserved, and you still hold both Japanese and U.S. citizenship.** Japanese law requires people with more than one nationality to choose one. The deadline changed with an amendment on April 1, 2022. People who have held both nationalities since before turning 18 must, in principle, choose by age 20. However, transitional measures apply to people who already held two nationalities before the amendment, so the deadline differs depending on their age and when they acquired the second nationality. Missing the deadline does not immediately end Japanese nationality. However, the Minister of Justice may send a written notice, and a person who does not choose within one month of receiving it loses Japanese nationality. Also note that filing a declaration choosing Japanese nationality does not necessarily end U.S. citizenship automatically. In that case, you must make efforts to renounce the foreign nationality.
- **The available records are incomplete or unclear.** These situations can arise when families have lived in multiple countries. Depending on the circumstances, it may be necessary to review family register records and determine whether any corrections or additional procedures are required.

## What changes depending on whether nationality was reserved

Whether nationality was reserved can significantly change the procedures your family needs to consider.

- **If nationality was properly reserved:** As long as your child reserved Japanese nationality and has not lost it since, no visa is needed to live, work or study in Japan.
- **If Japanese nationality was lost:** Your child needs a [status of residence](/visa/guides/how-to-move-to-japan/) to live in Japan, as other foreign nationals do. However, a person born as the child of a Japanese national may be able to apply for the **Spouse or Child of Japanese National (日本人の配偶者等)** status if they meet the requirements, such as the parent-child relationship.
